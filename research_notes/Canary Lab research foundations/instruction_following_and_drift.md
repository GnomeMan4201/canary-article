# Instruction-following evaluation and instruction adherence degradation: foundations for the Canary Lab instruction canary

Scope note: compiled 2026-11-24. Verification levels: **[fetched]** means the arXiv abstract page or full text was read this session. **[recall]** means the citation and finding come from prior knowledge of the paper and the abstract was not re-read this session; check the number before publishing it. Canary Lab context: one exact-format marker in AGENTS.md, checked on the final response, 30 runs, 15/15 markers exact, 30/30 rule checks passing (docs/experiment.md, docs/follow-up-protocol.md).

## (a) Verifiable instruction-following benchmarks and how format instructions are scored

### Takeaway
The standard methodology is IFEval-style **programmatic verification** of atomic, objectively checkable instructions, reported at four granularities: prompt-level vs instruction-level, crossed with strict vs loose. Canary Lab's scorer is a strict, single-instruction, prompt-level check. The literature treats loose scoring as a complement that reduces false negatives but adds false positives. It also shows that models can be tuned to the IFEval instruction set, so a novel instruction (like a random-token marker) is a cleaner probe than a familiar one.

### Cited Findings
- **IFEval**: Zhou, Lu, Mishra, Brahma, Basu, Luan, Zhou, Hou. "Instruction-Following Evaluation for Large Language Models." arXiv:2311.07911 (2023). 25 types of "verifiable instructions" (e.g., "write in more than 400 words", "mention the keyword AI at least 3 times"), 541 prompts. Some types are positional/format instructions close to Canary Lab's: "Start with / End with", "end your email with: P.S. …", "wrap your entire response with double quotation marks", "first repeat the request word for word without change, then give your answer", "Entire output should be wrapped in JSON format". **[fetched, full text]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911)
- IFEval reports four metrics: (1) prompt-level strict accuracy, the % of prompts where *all* instructions are followed; (2) instruction-level strict accuracy, the % of individual instructions followed; (3) and (4) the same two under the loose criterion. **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911)
- IFEval's **loose criterion** counts an instruction as followed if *any* of several transformed responses passes. The transformations are: remove markdown font modifiers (`*`, `**`); remove the first line (to skip intros like "Sure, here it is:"); remove the last line (to skip outros like "Hope it helps."); and combinations of these. The paper's motivating example is that "**P.S. I do like the cake**" fails a strict string match only because of bold markers. The authors warn that loose scoring "is likely to introduce false positives." For example, deleting the first line can make a response that misses a word-count instruction look compliant. So they "consider this loose criterion as a complement to the original criterion." **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911)
- IFEval baseline numbers (Table 3): GPT-4 (Nov 2023) scored 76.89% prompt-strict, 83.57% inst-strict, 79.30% prompt-loose, 85.37% inst-loose. PaLM 2 S scored 43.07 / 55.76 / 46.95 / 59.11. The strict-to-loose gap was about 2-4 points. **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911)
- **FollowBench**: Jiang et al. "FollowBench: A Multi-level Fine-grained Constraints Following Benchmark for Large Language Models." arXiv:2310.20410, ACL 2024. It covers five constraint categories (content, situation, style, format, example) and builds a "multi-level" ladder by adding one constraint per level (levels 1-5). Metrics: Hard Satisfaction Rate (HSR, all constraints met), Soft Satisfaction Rate (SSR, mean per-constraint satisfaction), and Consistent Satisfaction Levels (CSL, how many consecutive levels a model satisfies). Performance falls as levels increase, and even the strongest models tested (GPT-4 era) were far from satisfying all five levels. **[recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410)
- **InFoBench**: Qin et al. "InFoBench: Evaluating Instruction Following Ability in Large Language Models." arXiv:2401.03601, Findings of ACL 2024. It introduces the Decomposed Requirements Following Ratio (DRFR): each instruction is broken into yes/no requirement questions (500 instructions, 2,250 decomposed questions), and an LLM judge (GPT-4) answers them. The paper argues this is more reliable and cheaper than holistic judging. **[recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601)
- **ComplexBench**: Wen et al. "Benchmarking Complex Instruction-Following with Multiple Constraints Composition." arXiv:2407.03978, NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks. It uses a hierarchical constraint taxonomy and four *composition* types (And, Chain, Selection, Nested), with rule-augmented LLM scoring and dependency-aware scoring, so a constraint nested under a failed branch is not credited. Models are notably weaker on Chain/Selection/Nested compositions than on simple And. **[recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03978)
- **IFBench**: Pyatkin et al. "Generalizing Verifiable Instruction Following." arXiv:2507.02833 (2025). It adds a set of new, out-of-distribution verifiable constraints and reports that models scoring well on IFEval's 25 constraints generalize poorly to unseen ones. This is evidence of overfitting to a familiar instruction set. **[recall; constraint count not re-verified]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02833)

### Inferences
- Canary Lab's marker is an IFEval "Start with"-type instruction scored at the strictest setting: exact line match, with no stripping of markdown and no skipping of an intro line. Under IFEval loose rules, a response that opens "Sure:" and has the marker on line 2, or that writes `**CANARY:…**`, would pass. **Protocol change:** keep strict as primary, but publish a pre-registered *secondary* loose score. Compute it with IFEval-like transforms: strip `*`/`**`/backticks, allow the marker on line 2 if line 1 is a short preamble, and ignore surrounding whitespace. The existing three-way classification (exact / token present but malformed / absent) is already more informative than IFEval's binary split. Map "malformed" explicitly onto "loose-pass, strict-fail".
- IFEval's prompt-level vs instruction-level distinction maps directly onto a multi-marker design. Report both "all markers exact" (prompt-level) and the fraction of markers exact (instruction-level).
- IFBench's overfitting result supports Canary Lab's use of a random 16-hex token. The *format* ("first line exactly X") is still a heavily trained IFEval pattern, though, so 15/15 compliance may reflect training on that pattern more than general attentiveness. **Protocol change:** consider at least one less-familiar marker format, for example a marker required at a specific *position inside a generated file*, or a conditional marker. Any new format needs separate calibration.
- InFoBench/ComplexBench imply an option to decompose: score the marker instruction as several sub-requirements (token correct, on the first nonempty line, nothing else on the line). That gives a graded measure instead of a binary one.

### Gaps
- I did not re-fetch the FollowBench, InFoBench, ComplexBench or IFBench abstracts this session. Exact dataset sizes and per-model numbers listed under [recall] should be checked before citation.
- No benchmark I found reports the specific failure rate of "first line must be exactly X" instructions in isolation. IFEval's per-category figure exists (Figure 2/3 of the paper), but I could not extract the numeric value from the PDF text.

## (b) Adherence when many instructions or constraints are stacked; quantitative models

### Takeaway
Compliance degrades steadily as simultaneous instructions increase. All-instructions-satisfied (prompt-level) accuracy falls much faster than per-instruction accuracy, roughly as a product of per-instruction rates. Instruction count alone predicts multi-instruction performance well. At high density, models show a **primacy bias** (earlier instructions are followed more), and failures are mostly **omissions** rather than malformed attempts.

### Cited Findings
- **ManyIFEval / StyleMBPP**: Harada et al. "When Instructions Multiply: Measuring and Estimating LLM Capabilities of Multiple Instructions Following." arXiv:2509.21051, Findings of EMNLP 2025. ManyIFEval extends IFEval to as many as 10 simultaneous instructions. StyleMBPP covers *code generation* with up to 6 style instructions. Performance "consistently and drastically degrades as the number of instructions increases." A logistic regression with instruction count as the explanatory variable predicts multi-instruction performance with about 10% error, even for unseen instruction combinations. Data and code: github.com/kenoharada/Multiple-Instructions-Following. **[fetched via search abstract]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051); [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/new-sigs/2025.findings-emnlp.896/)
- A third-party leaderboard reports DeepSeek-V3 at 88.5% MMLU but only 19% prompt-level accuracy with 10 instructions on ManyIFEval. **[secondary source; not verified against paper tables]** — [alphaXiv benchmark page](https://alphaxiv.org/benchmarks/the-university-of-tokyo/manyifeval)
- "Curse of instructions" label: later papers describe ManyIFEval as showing a "curse of instructions". I believe an earlier version of the same work circulated as an ICLR 2025 OpenReview submission titled "Curse of Instructions: Large Language Models Cannot Follow Multiple Instructions at Once." To my recollection, it stated that the all-instructions success rate approximates the per-instruction success rate raised to the power of the number of instructions (P_all ≈ p^n). **[recall; I could not confirm the OpenReview version or the exact wording this session]** — related published version: [arXiv 2509.21051](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051)
- **IFScale**: Jaroslawicz, Whiting, Shah, Maamari (Distyl AI). "How Many Instructions Can LLMs Follow at Once?" arXiv:2507.11538 (2025), NeurIPS 2025 listing. The task is a business report that must include N keywords, with N from 10 to 500 in steps of 10, tested on 20 models from 7 providers with 5 seeds per density. The best models reach only about 68% at 500 instructions (gemini-2.5-pro-preview 68.9%). **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11538v1); [NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/122417)
- IFScale identifies three degradation shapes:
  - Threshold decay: stable, then a steep and variable drop (gemini-2.5-pro, o3).
  - Linear decay (gpt-4.1, claude-3.7-sonnet).
  - Exponential decay: a sharp early drop to a floor of about 7-15% (gpt-4o, llama-4-scout, claude-3.5-haiku).

  **[fetched]** — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11538v1)
- IFScale **primacy effect**: models increasingly favor earlier instructions as density rises. The bias is minimal at low density, peaks around 150-200 instructions, and shrinks at 300+ as failure becomes uniform. **Omission** errors increasingly dominate *modification* errors (near-miss variants). llama-4-scout's omission:modification ratio reaches 34.88 at 500 instructions. Reasoning models' latency increases sharply with density (o4-mini goes from 12.4 s at 10 instructions to 436 s at 250). **[fetched]** — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11538v1)
- FollowBench's level 1-5 ladder (see (a)) and ComplexBench's composition types show the same monotone decline as constraints stack, and nested or conditional constraints are harder than conjunctive ones. **[recall]** — [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410); [ComplexBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03978)
- **AgentIF** (agentic prompts): Qi et al. "AgentIF: Benchmarking Instruction Following of Large Language Models in Agentic Scenarios." arXiv:2505.16944, NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks. It has 707 human-annotated instructions from 50 real agentic applications. Instructions average 1,723 words (max 15,630) and carry about 11.9 constraints each, including tool specifications and conditional constraints. Scoring is code-based, LLM-based, or hybrid. Models "perform poorly", especially on complex constraint structures and tool specifications, and performance drops as instructions get longer. **[fetched via search abstract]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16944); [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/51bb3a8a33610a25aae074bfc51b1b1f-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html)

### Inferences
- **Quantitative model for Canary Lab:** if marker compliance is p per run and independent of other instructions, the probability of all k markers being exact is p^k. Under that baseline, a multi-marker study expects all-markers-exact to fall geometrically with k even if nothing degrades. Pre-register the **independence baseline p^k**, with p estimated from single-marker runs, and test whether observed joint compliance falls *below* it (correlated failure, the signal a canary hopes to catch) or *above* it (failures cluster in a few "inattentive" runs). ManyIFEval's logistic-in-count model is an alternative baseline.
- With 15/15 exact, the rule-of-three 95% upper bound on the per-run marker-omission rate is about 3/15 = 20%. The Wilson upper bound is similar, about 20%. The main study cannot rule out omission rates up to about one in five. This should go in the article alongside "15/15".
- **Protocol change (distractor/load arm):** add a condition with many *additional* checkable, low-stakes instructions in AGENTS.md (e.g., 10, 25, 50 keyword or format rules), with the marker held constant. IFScale and ManyIFEval predict this is the most reliable way to produce marker variance without changing model or task. It is a different factor from 02a-02c and must be its own arm.
- IFScale's primacy finding implies that in a dense instruction file, marker position matters. Place markers early, middle and late and treat position as a factor (see (e)). IFScale's omission-dominance implies the "absent" category, not "malformed", will dominate failures. The existing absent/malformed split is the right one to keep.
- For code agents, StyleMBPP is the closest analogue: style instructions on generated code. It supports adding *code-embedded* markers (e.g., a required comment in each new function) as a second marker channel.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly tests whether failure on a trivial marker instruction *correlates* with failure on substantive instructions within the same response. All these benchmarks score instructions independently. This is exactly Canary Lab's open question, and the literature does not answer it.
- The exact functional form from the earlier "Curse of Instructions" version (p^n) is unverified this session.

## (c) System-prompt / developer-instruction adherence and instruction hierarchy

### Takeaway
Models are trained to rank system/developer instructions above user and tool content (OpenAI's instruction hierarchy). Benchmarks show this works when instructions align but degrades sharply under conflict and over multi-turn conversations. Where an AGENTS.md file sits in that hierarchy depends on the harness. Recent coding-agent evidence shows agents treat instruction files as authoritative even over contradicting code.

### Cited Findings
- **Instruction hierarchy**: Wallace, Xiao, Leike, Weng, Heidecke, Beutel. "The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions." arXiv:2404.13208 (OpenAI, 2024). It proposes a priority ordering (system > user > model outputs/tool results). Training data is synthesized for *aligned* lower-priority instructions (follow them) and *misaligned* ones (ignore them). Applied to GPT-3.5, the training substantially increased robustness to prompt injection and system-prompt extraction, including attack types not seen in training, with minimal degradation of standard capabilities. **[recall; exact robustness percentages not re-verified]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208)
- **IHEval**: Zhang et al. "IHEval: Evaluating Language Models on Following the Instruction Hierarchy." arXiv:2502.08745, NAACL 2025 (oral). The hierarchy is system message > user message > conversation history > tool outputs, tested on 3,538 examples across nine tasks, in aligned and conflicting cases. "All evaluated models experience a sharp performance decline when facing conflicting instructions." The best open-source model reaches only 48% on conflict resolution. **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08745)
- **SysBench**: Qin et al. "SysBench: Can Large Language Models Follow System Messages?" arXiv:2408.10943 (2024). It has 500 manually constructed system messages built on six constraint types, paired with multi-turn user conversations. It names three failure modes: "constraint violation, instruction misjudgement and multi-turn instability." It reports at constraint, instruction and session level (CSR/ISR/SSR, to my recollection; the metric names were not on the abstract page). **[fetched abstract; metric definitions recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10943)
- **Control Illusion**: Geng, Li, Mu, Han, Baldwin, Abend, Hovy, Frermann. "Control Illusion: The Failure of Instruction Hierarchies in Large Language Models." arXiv:2502.15851, AAAI-26 (Proc. AAAI 40(36):30816-30824). Six SOTA models struggle to prioritize consistently "even for simple formatting conflicts". System/user separation "does not reliably establish an instruction hierarchy". Models show strong inherent biases toward certain constraint types regardless of priority. Societal framings (authority, expertise, consensus) influence behavior more than system/user roles. **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15851)
- **Coding-agent hierarchy evidence**: Xu and Wu. "Stale Instruction Files Override the Code in Coding Agents: A One-Sentence Fix." arXiv:2610.30571 (Oct 2026). The setup is 40 Python projects where CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md prescribes one convention and the code uses another, with Claude Code and Codex CLI across 320 runs. Without intervention, 90.1% of choices followed the stale instruction file. Claude Code noticed the conflict in 79/80 runs but still followed the file. Codex rewrote existing code to match the file in 20/80 runs, breaking project tests in 17 of those. One sentence in the user-level instruction file giving the existing code precedence raised code-following from 4.2% to 91.4% and test pass rate from 89.4% to 100%. The authors attribute the effect to the file arriving as an *instruction* and the code as *tool output*. **[fetched abstract]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30571)
- **What the model actually sees**: de Sant'Anna Paixão, Tavares, Almeida, Ahmed. "Hidden in Plain Sight: an Empirical Study about The Instruction Stacks of Coding Agents." arXiv:2610.37332 (Oct 2026). It captured the first model request across 1,886 releases of Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, Qwen Code and OpenCode. Tool definitions dominate the latest requests of three agents. Concentrated revisions change instructions on tool use, attribution and coding style. Only 48% of change units explain their motivation. The authors recommend documenting captured instruction stacks alongside model and release, because the model name or system prompt alone is "an incomplete picture." **[fetched abstract]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37332)

### Inferences
- Canary Lab's marker is a *formatting* instruction from a project file. Control Illusion shows formatting constraints are exactly where hierarchy enforcement is inconsistent, and that constraint *type* biases can override priority. **Protocol change:** add a conflict probe. A task prompt or tool output (e.g., a README or code comment in the fixture) asks for a different first line, and the measure is which instruction wins. This tests the AGENTS.md hierarchy position directly. It is a different construct from attention and should be a separately labeled arm.
- Xu & Wu and Paixão et al. imply that the **harness's instruction stack is an uncontrolled variable** and changes between CLI releases. **Protocol change:** capture and hash the full first model request, or the effective instruction stack if the harness exposes it, for every run. Treat a CLI version change as a new cohort. This extends the existing "record effective instruction sources" requirement.
- SysBench's "multi-turn instability" and IHEval's history tier point to the drift arm in (d): a project-file instruction may sit at system/developer level, or may be injected as a user-turn message depending on the harness. Record which.

### Gaps
- I found no published account of where a given coding harness places AGENTS.md in the role hierarchy (system vs user message) that is current as of Nov 2026. Paixão et al. capture stacks, but I read only the abstract.
- Wallace et al.'s exact robustness numbers were not re-verified.

## (d) Degradation over long contexts and multi-turn conversations (drift, lost-in-the-middle, position effects)

### Takeaway
Three robust findings bear on the canary design. Instruction adherence decays over dialog turns, and attention-decay explanations have been proposed. Retrieval and use of information is position-dependent (U-shaped: best at start and end). Effective context is much shorter than advertised context. In coding agents specifically, a controlled 2026 study found within-session decay in adherence to a trivial annotation instruction (about 5.6% lower odds per generated function). That is the closest existing analogue to a repeated canary.

### Cited Findings
- **Instruction (in)stability**: Li, Liu, Bashkansky, Bau, Viégas, Pfister, Wattenberg. "Measuring and Controlling Instruction (In)Stability in Language Model Dialogs." arXiv:2402.10962, COLM 2024. The benchmark uses self-chats between two instructed chatbots. It finds "significant instruction drift within eight rounds of conversations" for LLaMA2-chat-70B and GPT-3.5, attributes this partly to attention decay over long exchanges, and proposes split-softmax as a mitigation that "compares favorably" with two baselines. **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10962)
- **LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation**: Laban, Hayashi, Zhou, Neville. arXiv:2505.06120 (May 2025). Across more than 200,000 simulated conversations, top open and closed LLMs perform significantly worse when a task is spread over multiple turns, with a 39% average drop across six generation tasks. The drop decomposes into a minor loss of aptitude and a large increase in *unreliability* (variance between runs). Models make early assumptions, attempt final answers prematurely, rely on those attempts, and "do not recover." **[fetched abstract]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)
- **Multi-IF**: He, Jin, et al. (Meta). "Multi-IF: Benchmarking LLMs on Multi-Turn and Multilingual Instructions Following." arXiv:2410.15553 (2024). IFEval is extended to 3-turn conversations in 8 languages (4,501 conversations, 14 LLMs). "All the models tested showed a higher rate of failure in executing instructions correctly with each additional turn." o1-preview averages 0.877 at turn 1 and 0.707 at turn 3. **[fetched]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15553)
- **MultiChallenge**: Sirdeshmukh et al. (Scale AI). "MultiChallenge: A Realistic Multi-Turn Conversation Evaluation Benchmark Challenging to Frontier LLMs." arXiv:2501.17399 (2025). It includes an "instruction retention" category, meaning instructions given early that must persist across later turns. Frontier models scored below 50% overall at release. **[recall; numbers not re-verified]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17399)
- **Lost in the Middle**: Liu, Lin, Hewitt, Paranjape, Bevilacqua, Petroni, Liang. "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts." arXiv:2307.03172, TACL 2024. On multi-document QA and key-value retrieval, performance is highest when the relevant information is at the beginning or end of the context and degrades significantly in the middle (U-shaped curve), even for long-context models. **[recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)
- **RULER**: Hsieh et al. (NVIDIA). "RULER: What's the Real Context Size of Your Long-Context Language Models?" arXiv:2404.06654, COLM 2024. It goes beyond needle-in-a-haystack with multi-needle, multi-hop tracing, aggregation and QA tasks. Despite near-perfect vanilla NIAH scores, models degrade substantially as length grows, and only about half of models claiming 32K+ context held satisfactory performance at 32K. **[recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654)
- **Context Rot**: Hong, Troynikov, Huber (Chroma). "Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance" (technical report, July 2025). Across 18 models, performance on even simple tasks degrades non-uniformly as input length grows, and distractors and needle-question similarity amplify the effect. **[recall; industry report, not peer-reviewed]** — [Chroma research](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)
- **Within-session decay in a coding agent (closest analogue)**: McMillan. "Instruction Adherence in Coding Agent Configuration Files: A Factorial Study of Four File-Structure Variables." arXiv:2605.10039 (May 2026). There were 1,650 Claude Code CLI sessions (primarily Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.6 cross-check) on two TypeScript codebases and five tasks, giving 16,050 function-level observations. The target instruction was in CLAUDE.md: every new or modified function body must begin with `// @tracked`, checked by AST. The per-run Instruction Compliance Rate is compliant functions divided by all functions.
  - In a mixed-effects model, each additional generated function was associated with about 5.6% lower odds of compliance (OR = 0.944, 95% CI lower bound 0.937).
  - The relationship is non-monotonic. All three CLI-matched model/codebase cells show negative slopes (e.g., OR 0.960, 0.971, 0.934).
  - This finding was identified post hoc, not pre-registered.
  - Compliance varied strongly by task, from 45.1% (T4) to 84.4% (T2).
  - With no configuration file, 0 of 338 and 0 of 544 functions carried the annotation (Wilson upper bounds about 1.1% and 0.7%). So spontaneous emission is about zero, the counterpart of a "no-marker" control.

  **[fetched, full text]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039)
- McMillan's related-work section notes that an informal blog describes a compliance curve that "collapses between turn 5 and turn 20". The paper chose to analyze within-session *function position* rather than turn count. **[fetched; the underlying blog was not checked]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039)

### Inferences
- Canary Lab measures one final-response marker per fresh single-task session. Every degradation result above concerns either *many* turns or *many* outputs per session. The 15/15 result sits in the regime where the literature predicts near-ceiling compliance. **Protocol change (drift arm):** in a multi-turn session, require the marker on every assistant final response, or after every meaningful edit as experiment.md already proposes. Plot exact-marker rate against turn index. Li et al. (drift within 8 rounds), Multi-IF (turn 1 to turn 3 decline) and Laban et al. (unreliability increase) all predict a detectable slope. Use mixed-effects or GEE models with a run random effect, as McMillan does, because checkpoints within one session are dependent.
- **Protocol change (repeated in-artifact marker):** McMillan shows that a trivial per-function annotation in CLAUDE.md yields variance (overall ICR about 64%, task range 45-84%) where a once-per-run marker gave none. Adding an AGENTS.md rule such as "every new or modified function must start with comment `# canary:<token>`" would give many observations per run. It would also measure compliance *inside the work product* rather than in the chat reply, and allow within-session decay curves. It can then be cross-tabulated against the hidden behavioral checks at function granularity. This is likely the single highest-value change for producing a marker/work disagreement.
- Laban et al.'s "unreliability" framing implies that run-to-run variance, not just mean compliance, is the quantity to report. Use repeated runs per task (already 5) and report dispersion.
- Lost in the Middle and RULER bear on marker *depth*: as the agent's context fills with tool output, an AGENTS.md instruction near the start of context moves further from the generation point. Record total context tokens at the final response and, if possible, at any compaction event, and use them as covariates.
- McMillan's task effect (45-84%) is larger than any structural effect. This supports the follow-up protocol's choice of reporting by task, and warns against pooling tasks.

### Gaps
- Exact numbers for Lost in the Middle, RULER and MultiChallenge are from recall and should be checked.
- I found no study of instruction-file adherence *across a context compaction or summarization event* in a coding agent. This is a genuine gap matching Canary Lab's planned compaction study.
- The Laban et al. mitigation results (recap, temperature) were not on the abstract page.

## (e) Does instruction position or salience matter (separate instructions file vs prompt; top vs bottom)?

### Takeaway
Evidence is mixed and depends on context length. In generic long-context QA, position effects are strong (U-shape). Vendor guidance recommends putting the query or instructions at the end of long contexts, or at both ends. But the only controlled study of position *within a coding agent's config file* (250-line CLAUDE.md, positions from line 2 to line 250) found only a small descriptive primacy pattern and no statistically detectable effect. File size, multi-file architecture and adjacent-file contradictions also showed no detectable effect. Wording/salience of the instruction may matter more than position: conditional phrasing is followed less.

### Cited Findings
- **McMillan 2026, position and size nulls**: The target instruction was placed at line 2, 63, 128, 187 or 250 of a 250-line CLAUDE.md. ICR was 67.7%, 63.2%, 64.0%, 64.0% and 61.8% respectively (weak primacy at the top, then flat). This was not significant after correction, and the largest pairwise contrast (about ±5.9 pp) rules out large structural effects. File sizes of 25/100/250/500 lines gave ICR 60.0% / 65.2% / 67.7% / 64.0%. Size and conflict nulls are supported by Bayes factors (BF10 0.05-0.10). Position and architecture nulls are "failures to reject" without Bayes-factor support. A contradicting AGENTS.md instruction changed ICR by +0.33 pp (C0 63.7% vs C1 64.1%, p = 0.91). Nested CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md architectures (A1/A2/A3) showed no detectable effect. The author notes that Claude Code reads root CLAUDE.md automatically but does not read AGENTS.md by default, so AGENTS.md had to be injected. The author also cautions that a trivial marker "is an easily-detectable proxy … rather than a measure of adherence in general", and that transfer of absolute rates to harder instruction classes is open. **[fetched, full text]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039)
- McMillan also reports that a sizable fraction of trials returned a clarifying question rather than code (e.g., 38 of 150 trials, 25.3%, in one block). This motivated a separate Code-Production Rate metric alongside compliance. **[fetched, full text; exact block not fully parsed]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039)
- **Policy wording**: Tsuchida, Fan, Shimari, Kula, Matsumoto. "Do Coding Agents Follow AGENTS.md? The Effect of Policy Wording on Instruction Compliance." arXiv:2611.04367 (Nov 2026; under review at IEICE). One policy in four phrasing styles was inserted into the agent context files of 86 benchmark tasks across four harness-model combinations. **Conditional** phrasing was followed less often than **Prescriptive** or **Explanatory** phrasing. Which of the latter two was best depended on the harness-model combination. **[fetched abstract; no percentages on abstract page]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04367)
- **Lost in the Middle** (U-shape: start and end privileged) — see (d). **[recall]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)
- **IFScale primacy**: earlier instructions are favored, peaking at 150-200 instructions — see (b). **[fetched]** — [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11538v1)
- **Vendor guidance**: OpenAI's GPT-4.1 prompting guide recommends, for long context, placing instructions at both the beginning and end of the provided context. If they appear only once, above the context works better than below. **[recall]** — [OpenAI Cookbook](https://cookbook.openai.com/examples/gpt4-1_prompting_guide). Anthropic's long-context tips recommend putting long documents at the top and the query at the end, and report that queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30% in tests. **[recall]** — [Anthropic docs](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/long-context-tips)
- Anthropic's Claude Code memory docs (already cited by Canary Lab) describe CLAUDE.md as context, not enforcement. McMillan cites practitioner guidance to keep CLAUDE.md "under approximately 300 lines"; his size null at 25-500 lines is in tension with that guidance. **[fetched, via McMillan]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039); [Claude Code memory docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)

### Inferences
- Within the AGENTS.md sizes Canary Lab uses, the best controlled evidence (McMillan) predicts a **small or null** effect of marker position. A position arm is therefore lower priority than a load/distractor arm or a repeated-marker drift arm. If run, use at least 3 positions (top/middle/bottom) in a padded file of realistic length (about 250 lines). Pre-register it as an equivalence or ROPE test, because a null is the likely outcome.
- **File vs prompt salience:** I found no controlled study comparing the same instruction in AGENTS.md vs the task prompt for coding agents. Hierarchy work (Wallace, IHEval, Xu & Wu) suggests that where the file is injected (system vs user role) is the variable that matters. **Protocol change:** an optional arm placing the marker in the task prompt instead of AGENTS.md, holding the text constant. This also serves as a positive control that the instruction is followable when maximally salient.
- Tsuchida et al. imply the marker's wording is a confound for any cross-study comparison. Freeze the exact wording (already done). Note that a *conditional* marker ("if you modified X, start with …") is a natural harder variant expected to lower compliance.
- McMillan's 0% no-config baseline is the analogue of Canary Lab's condition A. It supports treating marker presence as evidence that the file was *loaded*, but not that it was *followed in substance*. The follow-up protocol already concedes this through its pilot-01 negative-control observation.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found for file-vs-prompt placement of the same instruction in a coding agent.
- I did not obtain the Tsuchida et al. percentages or the specific harnesses.

## (f) Instruction following in agentic and coding-agent settings (AGENTS.md / rules files), 2024-2026

### Takeaway
A fast-growing 2025-2026 software-engineering literature now studies agent context files directly. Agents "follow ACF guidance more often than not", but adherence is sharply bimodal by instruction. Context files cost tokens without reliably improving task success. Stale instructions override code. Trivial-annotation compliance in Claude Code is only about 64% at the function level and decays within sessions. Canary Lab's 15/15 final-response result is consistent with this literature, because one end-of-run format instruction is the easiest case.

### Cited Findings
- **Agent READMEs**: "Agent READMEs: An Empirical Study of Context Files for Agentic Coding." arXiv:2511.12884 (Nov 2025). It analyzed 2,303 context files from 1,925 repositories. Developers prioritize functional context: test procedures (75.9%), implementation details (70.8%), architecture (68.1%). It is descriptive only and does not test compliance. **[search abstract; authors not captured]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12884)
- **Evaluating AGENTS.md**: Gloaguen, Mündler-Sasahara, Müller, Raychev, Vechev. "Evaluating AGENTS.md: Are Repository-Level Context Files Helpful for Coding Agents?" arXiv:2602.11988 (Feb 2026; v3 Sep 2026). On SWE-bench with LLM-generated context files, plus a new set of issues from repos with developer-committed files, context files "do not generally improve task success rates" and raise inference cost by more than 20% on average. Agents *do* follow context-file instructions well. Repository overviews were not helpful, and context files are best used for non-standard practices. **[fetched abstract]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988)
- **Do They Do as They Are Told?**: Voria, Kashiwa, Catolino, Palomba. arXiv:2611.13709 (Nov 2026). From real AGENTS.md-style snapshots and linked agent-generated commits, explicit instructions were turned into executable measurement scripts. Agents follow ACF guidance "more often than not". Adherence is sharply split between instructions followed consistently and instructions never respected, and varies by instruction category and by agent. **[fetched abstract; no numbers on abstract page]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13709v1)
- **McMillan 2026** (trivial-annotation factorial in Claude Code), **Tsuchida et al. 2026** (wording), **Xu & Wu 2026** (stale files override code), **Paixão et al. 2026** (instruction stacks) — see (c)-(e). — [2605.10039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039); [2611.04367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04367); [2610.30571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30571); [2610.37332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37332)
- **AgentIF** (long agentic system prompts, about 11.9 constraints each; poor performance on tool-spec and conditional constraints) — see (b). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16944)
- Another search hit, arXiv:2611.02383, appeared relevant but its title was not captured. **[unverified; not read]** — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02383)

### Inferences
- Voria et al.'s bimodality ("followed consistently" vs "never respected") suggests per-instruction compliance is largely a property of the *instruction*, consistent with McMillan's large task effects. **Protocol change:** if Canary Lab uses several markers, vary marker *type* (final-response line, code comment, commit-message trailer, file header) rather than only position, and expect type to dominate.
- Gloaguen et al. (good instruction-following, no success gain) and Canary Lab main-01 (15/15 markers, 30/30 checks) jointly suggest that in easy, short, single-task settings both signals sit at ceiling. Variance requires load (more instructions), length (more turns and outputs), or harder or conflicting instructions. This is where the follow-up arms should go.
- Xu & Wu's mechanism (instruction file outranks tool output) predicts that a marker in AGENTS.md is *more* robust than the code-level rules learned from reading the fixture. That asymmetry could produce "marker present, rule failed" (false reassurance) cells, particularly in 02c's refactor task. Report those cells prominently.
- Paixão et al. imply that published results should include the captured instruction stack hash and CLI version per run. Harness updates silently change what surrounds AGENTS.md.

### Gaps
- Most 2026 coding-agent papers were read at abstract level only. Per-agent and per-category compliance numbers for Voria et al. and Tsuchida et al. were not obtained.
- I found no peer-reviewed venue publication yet for most of the 2026 arXiv cs.SE papers. Treat them as preprints.
- I found no study that pairs a *canary marker* with *independent behavioral tests* to measure whether marker omission predicts substantive rule violation. Canary Lab appears to occupy that niche. McMillan explicitly treats his marker as a proxy and does not test it against other rule compliance.
